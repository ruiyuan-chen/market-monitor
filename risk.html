Drawdown risk models

Three models estimate the chance that the S&P 500 closes at least 3% below today's close within the next 5 or 21 trading days: the author's turbulence model, which looks for stress inside the market, and two simple benchmarks that use only volatility. All three are retrained after every close and tested the same way.

      Teaching notes How the models work and how to judge them

      The turbulence model

      About 490 large U.S.-listed stocks are grouped into 10 clusters of stocks that move together, using two years of daily returns. Financial turbulence measures how far each cluster has decoupled from the S&P 500 over the past month: the leftover volatility of a regression of the index on the cluster, scaled by how weakly they correlate. Internal turbulence averages six breadth signals: new 52-week lows, the share of stocks below their 50- and 200-day averages, decliners, the Arms index and the dispersion of returns. Realized volatility is how much the S&P 500 itself has moved over the past month, annualized. A logistic regression turns these seven inputs into a probability.

      The two benchmarks

      VIX only and Volatility only are the same logistic regression with a single input: the VIX, which is the options market's own forecast of volatility over the next 30 days, or the past month's realized volatility. Volatility clusters, so both are hard to beat. If the turbulence model can't beat them, its extra inputs aren't adding information.

      Out of sample

      The track record, scorecard and trading results come from a walk-forward test. Each month the clusters are re-formed and every model is refit using only data available at that time, then it predicts the following month. This guards against look-ahead bias, the most common way backtests overstate skill.

      Reading the scorecard

      AUC is the chance that a randomly chosen day before a drawdown gets a higher probability than a randomly chosen calm day; 0.5 is a coin flip. Brier skill compares the probabilities with always predicting the usual rate: above zero is better. The 3% target is whether the index closes at least 3% below today's close at any point in the horizon. Such dips are common over a month, so compare each probability with its usual rate, which is what the risk level does.

      Reading the trading results

      Each rule holds the S&P 500 (SPY, dividends reinvested) and moves to cash, earning the T-bill rate, at the close of any day its model reads High, then earns the next day's return. Trading costs are included; the after-tax column assumes a 30% rate on short-term gains and 15% on buy and hold's gain at the end. The 90% range is a block bootstrap of the daily return gap: if it includes zero, the gap could be luck. High-risk days are high-volatility days, and they contain the market's best days as well as its worst, which is why stepping aside on them has usually cost money.

      Limits

      Large drawdowns are rare and overlapping windows mean far fewer independent events than days, so all the scores are noisy. The stock list is today's large companies, which adds survivorship bias to the history. This is a teaching and research tool, not investment advice.
