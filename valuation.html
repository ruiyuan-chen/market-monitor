    Teaching notes CAPE and the equity risk premium

    What CAPE is

    CAPE (also called the Shiller P/E or P/E10) divides the S&P index by the average of the past 10 years of inflation-adjusted earnings. Averaging over a decade smooths out the business cycle: in a recession, one year's earnings collapse and make the ordinary P/E look misleadingly high.

    What it predicts and what it doesn't

    Historically, high CAPE readings were followed by lower average returns over the next 10 years, with a lot of scatter around that pattern. CAPE says almost nothing about the next month or year, so it is a poor market-timing tool. Some argue that changes in accounting rules, buybacks and the index's tilt toward high-margin technology firms have raised its "normal" level.

    Earnings yield and excess CAPE yield

    The earnings yield is 1 ÷ CAPE, the real earnings stocks "pay" per dollar of price. Subtracting the 10-year TIPS real yield gives the excess CAPE yield, a rough estimate of the extra return stocks offer over safe real bonds (the equity risk premium).

    References: Campbell, J. Y., and R. J. Shiller (1988), "Stock Prices, Earnings, and Expected Dividends," Journal of Finance; Campbell and Shiller (1998), "Valuation Ratios and the Long-Run Stock Market Outlook," Journal of Portfolio Management; Shiller, R. J., Irrational Exuberance.

      Teaching notes What each factor captures

      Mkt−RF: the market

      The return on all U.S. stocks minus the one-month Treasury bill rate, i.e. the equity premium actually earned.

      SMB: size

      Small minus big: small-cap stocks minus large-cap stocks.

      HML: value

      High minus low: stocks with high book-to-market (cheap, "value") minus low book-to-market ("growth").

      RMW: profitability

      Robust minus weak: firms with high operating profitability minus firms with low profitability.

      CMA: investment

      Conservative minus aggressive: firms that grow their assets slowly minus firms that invest heavily.

      Mom: momentum

      Recent winners minus recent losers, ranked on returns from 12 months to 1 month ago.

      How to read the numbers

      Except for the market, each factor is a long-short "paper" portfolio, so its return is a spread between two groups of stocks. The figures ignore trading costs. The Sharpe ratio is the annualized average return divided by annualized volatility. Factor premia vary a lot over time: value had a long drawdown after 2007, and momentum has crashed sharply in market rebounds such as 2009.
