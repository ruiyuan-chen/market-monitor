        Teaching notes Yield curves, real rates and spreads

        The federal funds rate

        The Federal Reserve's policy rate is the overnight rate at which banks lend reserves to one another. The Fed sets a target range, and the effective rate shown here trades inside it. Short-term Treasury bill yields follow it closely.

        The yield curve

        The curve plots Treasury yields against maturity. It usually slopes upward because investors want extra yield to lock money up for longer (the term premium) and because markets expect rates to rise over time.

        Inversions and recessions

        When short rates exceed long rates (a negative spread), the curve is "inverted". This usually means markets expect the Fed to cut rates as the economy weakens. The 10-year minus 3-month spread turned negative before every U.S. recession since the late 1960s, though the lead time varies and it has given false alarms.

        Real yields and breakevens

        TIPS pay a real (inflation-adjusted) yield. The nominal 10-year yield minus the TIPS yield is the breakeven inflation rate, a market-based gauge of expected inflation that also includes some risk premia.

        Credit spreads

        The high-yield spread is the extra yield on below-investment-grade ("junk") corporate bonds over Treasuries. It widens when investors fear defaults and is a useful gauge of financial stress.

        The economy

        Inflation, jobs and growth, the data that drive Fed policy and corporate earnings. These series are monthly or quarterly and published with a lag.

            CPI vs. PCE

            The Consumer Price Index (BLS) and the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index (BEA) both measure inflation. They differ in their weights and coverage. The Fed's 2% target is defined in terms of PCE, which usually runs a little below CPI.

            Core inflation

            "Core" leaves out food and energy, whose prices swing a lot. It is a better guide to where inflation is heading, not a measure of the cost of living.

            Jobs

            The unemployment rate comes from a household survey. Payroll job gains come from a survey of employers, and both are released on the first Friday of most months. Payroll figures are revised in the following months, sometimes by a lot.

            GDP growth

            Real GDP growth is quoted as a seasonally adjusted annualized rate: the quarter's growth compounded as if it continued for four quarters.

            Recession shading

            The shaded periods are recessions as dated by the NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee, which announces dates months after the fact.
