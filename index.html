      Teaching notes Indices, volatility and drawdowns

      What an index measures

      An index tracks the value of a basket of stocks. The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite weight each company by its market value, so the largest firms move them the most. The Dow Jones Industrial Average holds just 30 stocks and weights them by share price, which is a historical quirk. The Russell 2000 tracks smaller U.S. companies.

      Price vs. total return

      The index levels here are price indices: they leave out dividends. A total-return index reinvests dividends, so over long horizons it grows noticeably faster than the price index.

      The VIX

      The VIX is the volatility of the S&P 500 over the next 30 days that option prices imply, quoted as an annualized percentage. Divide by √12 to get a typical one-month move: a VIX of 16 implies roughly ±4.6%. It tends to jump when stocks fall, which is why it is called the fear gauge.

      Implied vs. realized volatility

      "Realized volatility" is how much the index actually moved over the past month, annualized. Implied volatility (VIX) is usually higher than what is later realized. Option sellers earn this gap, known as the variance risk premium, as compensation for crash risk.

      Drawdown

      Drawdown is how far the index sits below its all-time closing high. By convention a decline of 10% is a "correction" and 20% a "bear market".

      Moving averages

      The 200-day moving average smooths out daily noise. Practitioners watch whether the index is above or below it as a rough trend signal. That is a description of market behavior, not a recommendation.

      Drawdown risk model

      A machine-learning model estimates the chance that the S&P 500 closes at least 3% below today's close within the next 5 and 21 trading days. It is retrained after every close.

        Teaching notes How the model works and how to judge it

        The inputs

        About 490 large U.S.-listed stocks are grouped into 10 clusters of stocks that move together, using two years of daily returns. Financial turbulence measures how far each cluster has decoupled from the S&P 500 over the past month: the leftover volatility of a regression of the index on the cluster, scaled by how weakly they correlate. Internal turbulence averages six breadth signals: new 52-week lows, the share of stocks below their 50- and 200-day averages, decliners, the Arms index and the dispersion of returns.

        The model

        A logistic regression turns the inputs into a probability. The target is whether the index closes at least 3% below today's close at any point in the next 5 or 21 trading days. Because 3% dips are common over a month, compare each probability with its usual rate, which is what the risk level does.

        Out of sample

        The track record and scorecard come from a walk-forward test. Each month the clusters are re-formed and the model is refit using only data available at that time, then it predicts the following month. This guards against look-ahead bias, the most common way backtests overstate skill.

        Reading the scorecard

        AUC is the chance that a randomly chosen day before a drawdown gets a higher probability than a randomly chosen calm day; 0.5 is a coin flip. Brier skill compares the probabilities with always predicting the usual rate: above zero is better. The baseline uses only the past month's volatility, a strong and simple benchmark because volatility clusters.

        Limits

        Large drawdowns are rare and overlapping windows mean far fewer independent events than days, so the scores are noisy. The stock list is today's large companies, which adds survivorship bias to the history. This is a teaching and research tool, not investment advice.

        References: Kritzman, M., and Y. Li (2010), "Skulls, Financial Turbulence, and Risk Management," Financial Analysts Journal; Kritzman, M., Y. Li, S. Page, and R. Rigobon (2011), "Principal Components as a Measure of Systemic Risk," Journal of Portfolio Management; López de Prado, M. (2018), Advances in Financial Machine Learning, on purged walk-forward testing.

        Teaching notes Sectors and the business cycle

        The 11 sectors

        S&P and MSCI sort companies into sectors under the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), based on how each company earns most of its revenue. Each sector fund here holds the S&P 500 members in that sector, weighted by market value.

        Cyclical vs. defensive

        Cyclical sectors (Consumer Discretionary, Industrials, Financials, Materials, Energy) are more sensitive to economic growth. Defensive sectors (Consumer Staples, Utilities, Health Care) sell things people buy in any economy. Comparing the two groups shows what the market expects about growth.

        Rates matter too

        Utilities and Real Estate pay steady cash flows and often trade like bonds, so they tend to fall when interest rates rise. Financials, especially banks, can benefit from a steeper yield curve.

        Teaching notes Market cap, P/E and concentration

        Market capitalization

        Share price × shares outstanding, the stock market's valuation of the whole company's equity. Index weights in the S&P 500 are based on market cap, adjusted for shares that actually trade (the "float").

        Concentration

        A handful of very large firms now make up a large share of the S&P 500's value, so their returns can dominate the index. That is why the index can rise while the "typical" stock falls.

        P/E ratios

        Trailing P/E = price ÷ the last 12 months of earnings per share. Forward P/E uses analysts' forecast of the next 12 months. A high P/E means investors pay more per dollar of current profit, usually because they expect faster growth or see lower risk. Firms with losses have no meaningful P/E ("n/m").

        Dividend yield

        Annual dividends per share ÷ price. Many large technology firms return cash mainly through buybacks rather than dividends, so their yield understates total payouts.

              Teaching notes Yield curves, real rates and spreads

              The federal funds rate

              The Federal Reserve's policy rate is the overnight rate at which banks lend reserves to one another. The Fed sets a target range, and the effective rate shown here trades inside it. Short-term Treasury bill yields follow it closely.

              The yield curve

              The curve plots Treasury yields against maturity. It usually slopes upward because investors want extra yield to lock money up for longer (the term premium) and because markets expect rates to rise over time.

              Inversions and recessions

              When short rates exceed long rates (a negative spread), the curve is "inverted". This usually means markets expect the Fed to cut rates as the economy weakens. The 10-year minus 3-month spread turned negative before every U.S. recession since the late 1960s, though the lead time varies and it has given false alarms.

              Real yields and breakevens

              TIPS pay a real (inflation-adjusted) yield. The nominal 10-year yield minus the TIPS yield is the breakeven inflation rate, a market-based gauge of expected inflation that also includes some risk premia.

              Credit spreads

              The high-yield spread is the extra yield on below-investment-grade ("junk") corporate bonds over Treasuries. It widens when investors fear defaults and is a useful gauge of financial stress.

              The economy

              Inflation, jobs and growth, the data that drive Fed policy and corporate earnings. These series are monthly or quarterly and published with a lag.

                  CPI vs. PCE

                  The Consumer Price Index (BLS) and the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index (BEA) both measure inflation. They differ in their weights and coverage. The Fed's 2% target is defined in terms of PCE, which usually runs a little below CPI.

                  Core inflation

                  "Core" leaves out food and energy, whose prices swing a lot. It is a better guide to where inflation is heading, not a measure of the cost of living.

                  Jobs

                  The unemployment rate comes from a household survey. Payroll job gains come from a survey of employers, and both are released on the first Friday of most months. Payroll figures are revised in the following months, sometimes by a lot.

                  GDP growth

                  Real GDP growth is quoted as a seasonally adjusted annualized rate: the quarter's growth compounded as if it continued for four quarters.

                  Recession shading

                  The shaded periods are recessions as dated by the NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee, which announces dates months after the fact.

                  Teaching notes Why other markets matter for stocks

                  Oil

                  WTI crude is the U.S. benchmark, quoted here as the front-month futures price. Oil prices feed straight into headline inflation and Energy-sector earnings. Sharp spikes have preceded several recessions.

                  Gold

                  Gold pays no income, so it tends to do well when real interest rates fall and during periods of fear. Central-bank purchases also move its price.

                  The dollar

                  The Dollar Index (DXY) measures the dollar against six currencies, mostly the euro. A stronger dollar lowers the dollar value of U.S. multinationals' foreign sales. EUR/USD is quoted as dollars per euro, so when it rises the dollar is weaker.

                  Bitcoin

                  Bitcoin trades around the clock and is far more volatile than stocks. Its price is quoted as of the time of the update.

                    Teaching notes CAPE and the equity risk premium

                    What CAPE is

                    CAPE (also called the Shiller P/E or P/E10) divides the S&P index by the average of the past 10 years of inflation-adjusted earnings. Averaging over a decade smooths out the business cycle: in a recession, one year's earnings collapse and make the ordinary P/E look misleadingly high.

                    What it predicts and what it doesn't

                    Historically, high CAPE readings were followed by lower average returns over the next 10 years, with a lot of scatter around that pattern. CAPE says almost nothing about the next month or year, so it is a poor market-timing tool. Some argue that changes in accounting rules, buybacks and the index's tilt toward high-margin technology firms have raised its "normal" level.

                    Earnings yield and excess CAPE yield

                    The earnings yield is 1 ÷ CAPE, the real earnings stocks "pay" per dollar of price. Subtracting the 10-year TIPS real yield gives the excess CAPE yield, a rough estimate of the extra return stocks offer over safe real bonds (the equity risk premium).

Shiller (1988), "Stock Prices, Earnings, and Expected Dividends," Journal of Finance; Campbell and Shiller (1998), "Valuation Ratios and the Long-Run Stock Market Outlook," Journal of Portfolio Management; Shiller, R. J., Irrational Exuberance.

                      Teaching notes What each factor captures

                      Mkt−RF: the market

                      The return on all U.S. stocks minus the one-month Treasury bill rate, i.e. the equity premium actually earned.

                      SMB: size

                      Small minus big: small-cap stocks minus large-cap stocks.

                      HML: value

                      High minus low: stocks with high book-to-market (cheap, "value") minus low book-to-market ("growth").

                      RMW: profitability

                      Robust minus weak: firms with high operating profitability minus firms with low profitability.

                      CMA: investment

                      Conservative minus aggressive: firms that grow their assets slowly minus firms that invest heavily.

                      Mom: momentum

                      Recent winners minus recent losers, ranked on returns from 12 months to 1 month ago.

                      How to read the numbers

                      Except for the market, each factor is a long-short "paper" portfolio, so its return is a spread between two groups of stocks. The figures ignore trading costs. The Sharpe ratio is the annualized average return divided by annualized volatility. Factor premia vary a lot over time: value had a long drawdown after 2007, and momentum has crashed sharply in market rebounds such as 2009.

                      References: Fama, E. F., and K. R. French (1993), "Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds," Journal of Financial Economics; Fama and French (2015), "A Five-Factor Asset Pricing Model," JFE; Jegadeesh, N., and S. Titman (1993), "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers," Journal of Finance; Carhart, M. M. (1997), "On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance," Journal of Finance.

                      About the data

                      Update schedule

                      An automated job runs every weekday at about 5:30 pm Eastern time, after the U.S. market close. Stock prices are end-of-day. The economic, valuation and factor data change only when their sources publish: monthly, with a lag.
